NATIONAL SCIENCE DAY FAIR · 2025

A Water Rocket & a Podium

One plastic rocket, an LPG leak detector, three hundred engineering stalls, and a second-place finish for Mathematics.

28 FEBRUARY 2025 · 6 MIN READ

Two weeks after the State Summit, the bottle was back. This time, however, there was a gas sensor, a buzzer, a circuit board, and a Mathematics team preparing for yet another competition. What started as a water rocket was slowly turning into something much bigger.

On 28 February 2025, we participated in the National Science Day Fair at the MGR Auditorium, Hindustan Institute of Technology and Science. The event was primarily organised for first-year B.Tech students, but our Mathematics Department had secured special permission for our seniors to participate as well.

I wasn't initially planning on participating. I was wandering around the academic block, looking at the preparations happening around campus, when I happened to pass by the senior classroom.

And that's when I heard the familiar call.

"Timothy! Perfect timing. Come here, hold this wire."

Apparently, my plans for the day had just changed.

01. Timothy! Perfect Timing. Come Here, Hold This Wire.

Inside the classroom, the seniors were working on the water rocket again. The previous version had already travelled to St. Joseph's, won the district competition, and made its way to the State Summit at Queen Mary's College. Now, they were bringing it back for the National Science Day Fair.

But this time, the project had another component.

A laptop sat on the desk, wires were scattered around, and a soldering iron was being used to work on a small green circuit board. Attached to it was an MQ-series gas sensor, along with a buzzer and an LED. The idea was to build a simple LPG gas-leak detection system.

The testing was rather practical. Someone would flick a disposable lighter, extinguish the flame immediately, and hold the nozzle near the sensor to expose it to the gas. The buzzer would sound, the red LED would light up, and someone would fan the sensor with a notebook until the alarm stopped.

Then came the adjustments, followed by another test, and then another.

I was handed a wire, some electrical tape, and a job. Before I knew it, I was helping with the setup rather than simply watching the seniors work.

The project now had two parts to demonstrate: the mechanics and mathematics behind the water rocket, and a working electronic system for detecting LPG leaks. One involved a bottle, water, pressure, and a launch pad. The other involved sensors, circuits, and a buzzer.

Somehow, both were going to sit under the same Mathematics Department stall.

02. Three Hundred Projects, One Mathematics Stall

On 28 February, the MGR Auditorium was filled with projects from across the engineering departments. There were hundreds of teams, with more than 300 projects in the exhibition.

Everywhere we looked, there was something interesting. Drones, robots, small vehicles, agricultural systems, biotechnology models, machine-learning projects, garbage sorting systems, and plenty of other engineering prototypes.

Some teams had clearly put enormous effort into building their models. A few had even represented HITS at events hosted by IITs. There was a lot to look at, and frankly, quite a lot to be impressed by.

Before the exhibition officially began, we waited through the opening proceedings and the introductory speeches. Once the formalities were over, teams settled into their stalls, arranged their projects, and began preparing for the judges and visitors.

Our stall had the LPG detection system on one side and the water rocket on the other. The calculations were written out, the launch equipment was ready, and the circuit was switched on.

Our faculty visited the stall, looked at what we had put together, and seemed pleased to see the project taking shape.

When the judges arrived, I was responsible for explaining the gas detection system. I demonstrated how the sensor responded to the gas, how the buzzer and LED were triggered, and how the system worked at a basic level.

The demonstration worked. The judges listened, asked a few questions, and moved on.

The rocket, however, needed a little more space. We couldn't exactly launch it inside the auditorium. So the seniors took the rocket, the launch pad, and the pump outside for the demonstration.

While they were demonstrating the rocket, the exhibition continued inside. The other projects were being judged, visitors were moving around, and we were trying to make the best of what we had.

The morning went on, and so did the judging.

03. When the Questions Get Difficult

As the judging progressed, we began to realise that presenting a working model was only one part of the competition. The questions could go much deeper than the demonstration itself.

We could explain the basic calculations, the pressure involved, the forces acting on the rocket, and the general idea behind its launch. But some of the questions required a deeper mathematical explanation than we had prepared.

At one point, our HOD brought a senior faculty member along to see the rocket. The professor began asking questions about the underlying mechanics and the derivations behind our calculations.

We tried explaining what we had done, but it became clear that our calculations were not detailed enough to answer everything that was being asked.

We had built the prototype, tested it, and calculated its expected performance. But we hadn't developed every part of the theoretical explanation to the level that the questions demanded.

The conversation was a little uncomfortable. Our professor seemed disappointed that we couldn't go further with the mathematical derivations. Eventually, they moved on to the other projects.

We were left thinking about the questions.

The rocket worked. We had calculations. We had a demonstration. But there was still a gap between what we had built and how deeply we could explain every part of it.

By around lunchtime, most of the main judging rounds had finished. The exhibition hall became much quieter. We stepped away from our stall, looked around at the remaining exhibits, and had lunch.

After the morning's judging, we weren't particularly confident.

The induction vehicle team had been impressive, and several other projects had clearly put in enormous effort. We had already faced some difficult questions, and we knew our mathematical derivations weren't as detailed as they could have been.

We were mostly waiting for the results.

04. "You're from Pure Mathematics? Then Explain This."

Later in the afternoon, a senior faculty member from Mechanical Engineering came walking through the exhibition area. He stopped at our stall, looked at the project, and then glanced at our department badges.

"Wait a minute. You're from Mathematics?"

"Yes, sir."

He looked at the rocket, the sensor circuit, and the rest of the setup.

"This is not what I usually expect from a Mathematics project. Explain what you've done."

The seniors began explaining the project again, this time walking him through the parts we had actually built: the pressure involved in the water rocket, the launch mechanism, the water-to-air arrangement, the basic calculations, and the gas sensor circuit.

He listened patiently, asked a few questions, and seemed genuinely interested in understanding how everything worked.

Eventually, he looked towards the launch setup.

"Let's see the rocket."

Our team lead took him outside, along with the launch pad and the bottle. We set everything up, pumped the rocket to the required pressure, and prepared for the demonstration.

The bottle launched cleanly into the air.

The professor watched its flight, and the demonstration seemed to leave a much better impression. For someone from Mechanical Engineering, seeing a working physical model backed by basic calculations was something he could appreciate firsthand.

He returned to the exhibition area, spoke with the organizers, and then went over to the judging desk to look at our evaluation.

We noticed him discussing our project and the marks we had received.

At some point, it became clear that he was advocating for us and that our scores were being reconsidered.

We looked at each other.

The mood around the stall changed almost immediately.

After the morning, when we had been questioning whether we had done enough, there was suddenly a little hope. We didn't know what the final result would be, but we were now waiting with a different kind of anticipation.

When the closing ceremony began, everyone gathered for the announcements.

The first prize went to the induction vehicle team — and honestly, they deserved it. Their project had been impressive, the effort was visible, and they had presented it well.

Then came the announcement for the runner-up.

Second Place — Department of Mathematics.

For a moment, we just looked at each other.

Then the excitement took over. The seniors started celebrating, hugging one another, and we made our way to the podium to collect the award.

A few weeks earlier, we had been standing at St. Joseph's with a plastic bottle, wondering whether it could compete against the other projects.

Now, at HITS, among hundreds of engineering exhibits, our department had secured another podium finish.

As we stood there with the certificate, one of the seniors turned towards me and smiled.

"See, Timothy? What did I tell you in January? When it flies, everything is good."

And honestly, I couldn't argue with that.

The project had brought us another win, and this time, I wasn't just watching from the sidelines. I had been part of the team.

By God's grace, the bottle had done it again. From the district competition to the State Summit, and now to a second-place finish at SDG 2025, it had somehow become the Mathematics Department's unlikely little champion.

And there was still another journey waiting for it.

But Gyan Disha? That's a story for another day.
