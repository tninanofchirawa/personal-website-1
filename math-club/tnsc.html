THE TAMIL NADU STATE MATHS TECH INNOVATION SUMMIT · 2025

The Bottle That Went to the State Summit

One water bottle, an FDP seminar, a district championship, and twenty-seven state awards.

FEBRUARY 2024 – FEBRUARY 2025 · 6 MIN READ

01. "Pack Your Bags. We're Building a Project."

It all started in January 2025 with an announcement from our seniors that sounded more like an order to mobilize than an academic assignment: "Pack your bags. We're building a project. A mathematics project."

As juniors, our immediate reaction was total confusion. How on earth do you build a tangible project out of mathematics? Notebook formulas, calculus lemmas, and integration steps don't come with hardware parts or assembly instructions.

Seeing our hesitation, our seniors stepped in with a ready-made solution: a completed, bound poster project on historical Indian mathematicians and their discoveries. It was a project they had built themselves back when they were in their first year for Mathophilia 2024, an event hosted by our own department. The instruction that came with it was brutally simple: take it and make do with it.

Holding that thick stack of charts and biographies, it felt like a safe, predictable option. But across the room, another group of seniors was huddled over a table, preparing something completely different.

Sitting in the middle of their workspace was an empty plastic soda bottle, a bicycle air pump, and a bucket of water.

02. "When It Flies, Everything Is Good"

While we were sitting at our desks sorting through our safe pile of chart papers and printouts, I kept glancing across the room. The seniors looked like they were running a backyard plumbing workshop rather than an academic research lab.

Curious, I finally walked over to their table.

"What is all this?" I asked.

One of them looked up, completely deadpan. "It's a rocket."

I stared at the soda bottle, then at the bicycle pump, and then back at him. I had never even heard of a "water rocket" before in my life. To me, it looked like a backyard toy. We were heading to an inter-college competition where students from other engineering institutions would be showing up with microcontrollers, complex sensors, and serious technical hardware. And our department was showing up with a discarded two-liter plastic bottle?

"This?" I said, not hiding my skepticism. "How is this going to compete against real projects? It looks useless."

The senior didn't get offended. He just leaned back, smiled, and gave me a piece of advice that I still remember:

"People don't necessarily give awards to the most complicated project. They remember the one that catches their attention. It's all about how you sell your project. And besides—when it flies, everything is good."

I just shook my head. "Okay, whatever."

I stood there watching them tinker, still thoroughly unconvinced. Seeing my doubt, one of the seniors added, "You know, this isn't the first time we've taken this to an event."

"What event?" I asked.

"Last year, at the SDG 2024 fair. We won first prize with it."

I looked down at the plastic bottle resting on the table. First prize? With this? I didn't believe a single word of it. It sounded like classic senior bragging meant to stop a junior from asking annoying questions.

Still, there was no point in arguing. I just nodded, said okay, and went back to my chart paper.

03. The Library Lectures and the Dark Horse

On the morning of 9 January 2025, our department group arrived at St. Joseph's College of Engineering for the district competition.

For those of us in the junior batch, the expectations were pretty modest: get our On-Duty (OD) attendance sorted, spend the day out with the seniors, and check out what an inter-college fest was like.

The event was hosted inside the college library. Looking around the project display area, it didn't take long to realize that our hand-me-down mathematician poster wasn't going to turn many heads. Seeing how things stood, a few of us talked it over.

As it turned out, the sessions happening inside the library's seminar hall were part of a Faculty Development Program (FDP). There were a few staff members and students from the other competitions in attendance. It was quite a mixed atmosphere, with some following the lectures and others seemingly more interested in their phones. I even spotted a couple of guys playing Free Fire!

Our professor knew there was an FDP happening—or at least, that was what we understood—and had given us strict instructions to make sure someone from HITS was present. If anyone came looking for our department or wanted to know what was happening with our delegation, there needed to be someone around to answer. So, while our two display teams took their stations outside, I settled into the hall.

The morning talks were surprisingly interesting. The first speaker opened with an unusual mathematical argument on why 1 was the greatest number rather than 0, using basic topology, infinite series, and sequences to build the discussion. Next came an engaging session on graph theory.

As the morning wore on, our classmates and seniors kept popping in and out between events, checking on the talks before heading back outside. Gradually, the chairs around me cleared out until I was the only one left from our college. Someone had to stay, take notes on the lectures, and maintain our department's presence in the hall, so I just stayed put and listened.

When the morning sessions finally wrapped up, we all regrouped for lunch.

If there is one thing St. Joseph's is known for, it's that they feed people well. We sat down with plates of good biryani and free ice cream, and that was when the table started buzzing with talk about how our department might actually win the competition.

I wasn't entirely convinced.

Earlier, while looking around the display area, I had noticed a considerable crowd gathered around another team's exhibit. A girl had arrived with a friend from another college, and her project had caught my attention. She had built something seriously impressive: a vertical, spinning roulette wheel, much like the prize wheels you see at a carnival, designed to demonstrate probability in real time.

What really stood out was the construction. It was a proper wooden wheel, made from carefully cut and joined pieces of plywood. Anyone who has worked with wood knows that even cutting, aligning, and gluing two pieces of plywood together takes considerable effort. Building an entire working vertical wheel on top of that was certainly an ambitious undertaking. Compared to our little plastic bottle, it was a much more elaborate project. It was hard not to wonder how we would fare alongside something like that.

"Are you guys sure?" I asked between bites of biryani. "Did you see that wooden wheel over there?"

That was when the seniors began filling me in on what had actually happened outside while I was sitting through the FDP.

They had mounted the bottle on the launch pad and pumped it with air. On their display sheet, they had already mentioned an expected flight height based on their pressure calculations. Naturally, the judges wanted to see whether the bottle could actually reach the height the team had predicted.

The first launch gave everyone a chance to see the bottle in action. They made a few adjustments, pumped it up again, and went for a second attempt. This time, the bottle shot up to approximately 25 feet, reaching the expected height. People had gathered around the launch area, curious to see whether the calculations would hold up in practice. With the target reached, someone suggested, "Why not one more?"

And so, they launched it a third time.

What had started as a project demonstration had turned into something people genuinely wanted to gather around and watch. The bottle wasn't just sitting on a display table anymore; it was actually flying, and the numbers on the sheet had something to show for themselves.

After lunch, my seniors and I returned to the seminar hall and settled into our seats together. Another session was underway, this time about how graphs are used in satellite imagery to enhance and sharpen images. We sat through the lecture, waiting for the competition results.

By then, the day was running short. The FDP and the mathematics competition were two separate proceedings, but both needed to be wrapped up. As the time for the closing ceremony approached, a senior dignitary from St. Joseph's College arrived. The lecture had to be brought to an early close, and the hall quickly shifted its attention to the announcements and prize distribution.

We were all sitting together when the results were announced.

Our department had secured First Place in the District!

For a project that had started with a plastic bottle, a bicycle pump, and a set of pressure calculations, it was quite an outcome. The seniors had managed to turn something that had initially looked like a backyard experiment into a district-winning mathematics project.

And that was only the beginning.

04. The State Summit and the 27 Winners

Winning at St. Joseph's punched our ticket to the grand finale: the Tamil Nadu State Maths Tech Innovation Summit at Queen Mary's College, Chennai, on 14 February 2025.

This time, there was no bus trip for us juniors. The department rule was firm: the seniors were travelling into the city, and the juniors had to stay behind on campus.

As it turned out, the summit wasn't another gruelling round of high-pressure project defences or courtyard launches. Because all the district-level competitions had already concluded, the state summit was essentially the grand gathering where all the qualifying district winners were brought together under one roof. The heavy lifting had already been done at St. Joseph's.

Our seniors had simply made the trip to attend the orientation and walk up to the stage.

When the ceremony began inside the auditorium, A. Kousalya and M. Dhilip stepped up under the banner of the National Council for Science and Technology Communication to officially receive our honours: the Best Project Award for Application of Mathematics in Water Rocket.

Now, if you look closely at the records, you'll find that the organizers actually awarded the "Best Project Award" to roughly twenty-seven different teams that day. But nobody in our department was splitting hairs.

A win is a win.

When the seniors returned to campus with the medals, the framed certificates, and the photographs from the auditorium, the mood had shifted completely. A project that had started as a discarded soft-drink bottle, a bicycle pump, and a hand-me-down poster had gone from an object of department scepticism to a district champion, and finally to a state-recognized award winner.

Back in the workroom, we put down the certificates and set the bottle on the table. The juniors were no longer just sceptical spectators, and the seniors were no longer just teasing us with backyard prototypes.

The state summit was behind us, and the next building process was already underway—leading straight into our SDG showcase on 28 February 2025, which continues in the next chapter.
